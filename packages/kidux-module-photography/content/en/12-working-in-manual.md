# Working in manual

In every mode but one, the camera measures the light again for every
picture, and it can change its mind. A friend in a white shirt walks into
the picture, and the camera, seeing so much white, makes the whole picture
darker. The sun comes out from behind a cloud for one picture and not the
next. You take ten pictures of the same thing and get ten different
brightnesses.

In **M**, **manual**, nothing changes until you change it. You set the
time and the aperture once, and every picture after that gets exactly the
same light. That makes manual the **most reliable mode there is, when the
light is steady and you can measure it**: a room lit by lamps, a cloudy
afternoon, a stage, a street at night, a table by a window. In steady
light:

- a row of pictures comes out all alike, which is what you want for a
  story, a set of pictures of your toys, or pieces of a panorama;
- snow stays white, and a black cat stays black, because the camera is not
  measuring them again and making them grey;
- a white shirt walking in changes nothing.

![The screen in M: you choose both the time and the aperture](screen-m.svg)

> [think] Manual is not harder because there are more knobs. It is easier,
> because the knobs stay where you put them.

## How to work in manual

1. **Measure once.** Point the camera at something middle: grass, a grey
   jumper, a pavement, in the same light as your picture. Or
   measure a spot on what matters most, as in chapter 10. Read the time
   and aperture the camera would choose, for instance in P.
2. **Choose what matters.** Set the time for the movement and the aperture
   for what is sharp, keeping the same light: one stop off one is one stop
   on the other (chapter 9). Set ISO yourself, not AUTO, so that it cannot
   change by itself.
3. **Take a test picture**, and look at it and at its **histogram**. Too
   dark? One stop longer, or one wider. Too bright? The other way.
4. **Leave it**, and take your pictures. Do not touch the dials.
5. **Measure again only when the light changes**: a cloud over the sun, a
   lamp switched on, walking from the sun into the shade, or the evening
   getting darker.

![The histogram after the test picture: a mountain with room on both sides](screen-histogram.svg)

And when not to use it: when the light changes all the time, clouds racing
across the sun, running in and out of a house, a party with flashing
lights. There, A or S, which measure every picture, are better.

::: try
1. Find a room lit by a lamp, and put a toy on a table.
2. In **P**, look at the time and aperture the camera chooses for the toy.
3. Set the mode dial to **M**, set the same time and aperture, and set ISO
   to a number, not AUTO.
4. Take a test picture and look at its histogram. Adjust until it is
   right.
5. Now take ten pictures of the toy as you move it around the room, with a
   white sheet of paper behind it in some of them. Do they all match?
6. Do the same in **P**. Did the white paper change the pictures?
:::

::: camera
Set the mode dial to **M** (manual, p. 18). The **control ring** sets the
time, from 1/2000 of a second to 60 seconds and **BULB**, and the **sub
dial** sets the aperture; the two can be swapped in the menus (manual,
p. 18 and 89). There is no exposure compensation in M: the time and the
aperture are the brightness (manual, p. 18).

Set ISO to a number in the live control, not AUTO (manual, p. 44). For the
screen to show how bright the picture will really be, **Live View Boost**
must be off in the menus, as it is unless an adult switched it on (manual,
p. 60). After the test picture, **INFO** shows its histogram, both while
you take pictures and when you look at them (manual, p. 14 and 28).
:::

## Keep trying

- On a cloudy day, measure once in M and take pictures all around the
  garden. Do they match better than in P?
- Take five pictures of the same view, a little to the side each time, in
  M, to put together as a panorama. Their skies will match.
- At a school play, measure the stage once and stay in M.

::: adult
This chapter teaches manual exposure as a working method: meter once on a
mid-tone or with spot metering, set time and aperture for their side
effects, fix ISO, verify with a test shot and its histogram, and re-meter
only when the light changes. In steady light it is the most consistent
mode, which is why it is used for studio work, panoramas, and events under
fixed lighting.

The XZ-2's manual does not describe a meter scale in M; the method here
relies on a reading taken in P or with spot metering, the screen's preview
with Live View Boost off, and the histogram, which all cameras offer.
:::
