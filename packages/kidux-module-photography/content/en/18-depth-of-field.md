# Depth of field: how much is sharp

When the camera focuses on a toy at one metre, only things at exactly one
metre are perfectly sharp. But a little in front and a little behind, the
picture does not suddenly go blurry: it gets softer slowly, so that a
slice of the world, in front and behind, still **looks** sharp. How deep
that slice is, is called the **depth of field**.

![A row of posts, the camera focused on the middle one. Above, with the aperture wide open: only a thin slice is sharp. Below, with a small aperture: a deep slice, every post sharp](depth-of-field.svg)

## Thin or deep

The aperture is what changes it most (chapter 7):

- A **wide** aperture, **F1.8**, makes the slice **thin**: the thing you
  focused on is sharp, and the background melts into a soft blur.
- A **small** aperture, **F8**, makes the slice **deep**: everything is
  sharp, from near to far.

::: compare
![A small aperture: the whole row of blocks is sharp](dof-small-aperture.jpg)

![A wide aperture, the same row: only the front blocks are sharp](dof-wide-aperture.jpg)
:::

Two other things make the slice thinner too: **getting closer** to what
you focus on, and **zooming in**. That is why a toy one hand away has a
blurry background, even at F8, and a mountain at F1.8 is still sharp all
over.

> [think] A face looks best when the slice is thin, with the background
> melted away. A landscape looks best when it is deep, sharp from the
> flowers at your feet to the hills.

## The blurry lights

Out of the sharp slice, every tiny point of light becomes a little
**disc**: the further from the slice, the bigger the disc. Fairy lights
far behind a toy, at a wide aperture, turn into soft round balls of
colour. Photographers call this **bokeh**, a Japanese word for blur.

![Christmas lights, far behind and out of focus on purpose: each one a soft disc](bokeh.jpg)

![A pepper mill sharp, and the salt shaker behind it soft, at a wide aperture](shallow-focus.jpg)

::: try
1. Set the camera to **A**.
2. Stand five pencils on a table in a row, one behind the other, and
   focus on the second one.
3. Take the picture at **F1.8**, and again at **F8**. How many pencils
   are sharp in each?
4. Put a toy 30 cm from the camera, with the garden far behind. Take it
   at F1.8 and at F8.
5. At night, put a toy in front of some fairy lights, a few metres
   behind it. Get close to the toy at F1.8. What happened to the lights?
:::

::: camera
In **A**, the control ring sets the aperture (manual, p. 16); the manual
says it in its own words: a smaller number makes the background softer,
a larger one makes more of the picture sharp. The screen shows the
picture at the widest aperture while you frame it; to see the depth of
field before the picture, give the **Fn1** button the job of **Preview**
in the menus, and press it: the aperture closes to the one you chose, and
the screen shows how much will be sharp (manual, p. 65).
:::

## Keep trying

- Photograph a friend's face, from near, at F1.8, with a busy background
  far behind. And at F8.
- Find a fence going away from you, and focus on a post in the middle at
  F1.8. Where does the blur start?
- Make fairy-light discs as big as you can. Do you need to be closer to
  the toy, or further away?

::: adult
This chapter explains depth of field: only one plane is in exact focus,
and the zone in front of and behind it that looks sharp depends on the
aperture, the distance to the subject and the focal length. In a
sentence for a curious child: a point out of focus is drawn as a small
disc, the circle of confusion, and a smaller aperture makes the discs
smaller, until they are too small to see.

The XZ-2's small sensor gives more depth of field than a large camera at
the same f-number, so the background melts most when the child is close
to the subject, at F1.8 and the wide end of the zoom. The pair is one row of
toy blocks taken twice from the same place, at a small and at a wide
aperture.
:::
