# Too dark, too bright

A photograph is like a bucket that fills with light while the shutter is
open. Too little light, and the picture is **dark**: we say it is
**underexposed**. Too much, and it is **washed out**, the bright parts
turned to plain white: it is **overexposed**. Just right, and it is
**well exposed**. Getting the right amount of light into the bucket is
called **exposure**, and the next chapters are all about it.

![Two stops too little light: dark](exposure-minus2.jpg)

![Just right](exposure-0.jpg)

![Two stops too much: washed out](exposure-plus2.jpg)
:::

Most of the time the camera works out the amount for you. It looks at how
bright the scene is and chooses. But it can be fooled, and it is fooled in
a way you can predict, which is the subject of chapter 10: snow comes out
greyish, a black cat comes out greyish, and a friend with a bright window
behind them comes out dark.

So every camera has a knob that says *"brighter, please"* or *"darker,
please"*. It is called **exposure compensation**, and it is marked with a
little square split in two, **+** and **−**. Each step of it is a
**stop**: **+1** lets in twice as much light as the camera wanted, **+2**
four times as much, and **−1** half.

![The screen with exposure compensation at +2.0: the picture is brighter, and the mark on the little scale has moved to the right](screen-compensation.svg)

## The histogram

The camera can also draw you a picture of the light, called a
**histogram**. Imagine every dot of your photograph sorted by how bright
it is: all the very dark dots piled up on the left, all the very bright
ones on the right, and the middle ones in the middle. The histogram is the
shape of those piles.

![The screen with the histogram: dark dots on the left, bright ones on the right](screen-histogram.svg)

> [think] A mountain squashed against the right side means lots of dots
> are as white as they can be, and their detail is gone. Squashed against
> the left, lots of dots are black. A mountain with room on both sides is
> usually a picture with nothing lost.

::: try
1. Put a toy on a table by a window.
2. Take a picture of it as the camera chooses.
3. Turn the exposure compensation to **+2** and take another. Then **−2**,
   and another. Then back to **0**.
4. Fill the whole picture with a white sheet of paper and take one. Now
   fill it with a dark jumper and take one.
5. Look at all of them, with the histogram. Is the paper white in its
   picture? Is the jumper dark?
:::

::: camera
Set the mode dial to **P**, program (manual, p. 15). The **sub dial**, the
ring round the arrow pad, changes the exposure compensation: turn it and
watch the number at the bottom of the screen, from −3.0 to +3.0, and the
little scale next to it (manual, p. 23). In iAUTO and in M there is no
exposure compensation (manual, p. 23).

The compensation stays where you left it, for the next picture and the
one after: put it back to **0.0** when you are done.

Press **INFO** to show the histogram while you take the picture (manual,
p. 14). With the histogram on, the camera can also paint red the parts
that will be too bright and blue the parts too dark.
:::

## Keep trying

- Take a picture of snow, or of a white wall in the sun, at 0 and at +1.
  Which one looks like what you saw?
- Take a picture of a black cat, a black dog or a black coat at 0 and at
  −1.
- Look at the histogram of a picture taken at night. Where is the
  mountain?

This chapter introduces exposure, under- and overexposure, exposure

Remind the child to set the compensation back to zero when done: a camera
left at +2 makes every later picture too bright.
