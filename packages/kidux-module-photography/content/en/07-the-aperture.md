# The aperture: how wide the window

Inside the lens there is a ring of thin metal blades, the **aperture**,
which leaves a hole in the middle. The blades can slide in to make the
hole smaller, or out to make it bigger, like the black middle of your eye,
the **pupil**: big in the dark, to catch all the light it can, and tiny in
the sun.

![Inside a small camera's lens: the aperture's blades, closed down to a small hole](aperture-real.jpg)

How wide the hole is, is written as a number with an **F** before it:
**F1.8**, **F2.8**, **F4**, **F5.6**, **F8**. And here is the strange part,
which everybody gets backwards the first time: **the small number is the
big hole**. F1.8 is wide open; F8 is a small hole.

![The aperture at F1.8, wide open; at F4; at F8, a small hole](aperture-blades.svg)

Why backwards? Because the number is really the bottom of a fraction, like
the shutter's. F8 means the hole is one eighth of a certain length of the
lens; F2 means one half. One eighth is small, one half is big. And every
step along that row, F2.8, F4, F5.6, F8, lets in **half** as much light
as the step before. That is one stop, the same stop as in chapter 5.

> [think] Your eye's pupil does this by itself. Stand in front of a mirror
> in a dim room, then switch on the light, and watch your pupils shrink.

The aperture does a second thing, besides letting in more or less light:
it changes **how much of the picture is sharp**, near and far. A wide
open hole makes only the thing you focused on sharp, and melts the
background into a soft blur. A small hole makes everything sharp, from
near to far. That is chapter 18, and it is one of the most beautiful
things a camera can do.

::: try
1. Set the camera so that you choose the aperture.
2. Look into the front of the lens while you turn the aperture from F1.8
   to F8 and back. Can you see the blades moving? Ask an adult to hold a
   torch beside you.
3. Take the same picture of a toy at **F1.8** and at **F8**.
4. Look at the time the camera chose for each one. Which was longer? Why?
:::

::: camera
Set the mode dial to **A**, aperture priority: you choose the aperture,
and the camera chooses the time (manual, p. 16). The **control ring**
changes the aperture, from F1.8 to F8, and the sub dial still changes the
exposure compensation. A smaller number, F2, softens the background; a
bigger one, F8, makes more of it sharp (manual, p. 16).

At the wide end of the zoom the lens opens to F1.8; zoomed all the way in,
only to F2.5 (manual, p. 92).

If it is so bright that even F8 and the shortest time let in too much
light, the time **blinks**; then switch on the **ND filter**, a dark glass
inside the camera that takes away three stops of light, in the live
control that OK opens (manual, p. 16, 35 and 92).
:::

## Keep trying

- Take a picture of a row of toys, one behind the other, at F1.8 and at
  F8. Which toys are sharp in each one?
- Look at a cat's eyes in a bright room and in a dim one. Its pupils are
  apertures too, shaped like slits.
- Make a hole with your fingers, as small as you can, and look through it
  at something near your face without your glasses, if you wear them.
  Does it look sharper?

::: adult
This chapter introduces the aperture and its f-number, why a smaller
number is a wider opening (the f-number is the focal length divided by the
opening's diameter), and the stops along the standard row. The photograph
of the blades is of another compact camera's lens, opened up; the XZ-2's
look much the same.

Depth of field, the aperture's second effect, is only mentioned here and
taught properly in chapter 18. The last *Keep trying* is the pinhole
effect: a small opening increases depth of field, even for an eye.
:::
