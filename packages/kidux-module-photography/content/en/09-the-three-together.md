# The three together

You know the three ways of letting light into a picture now:

- **The time**, how long the shutter is open.
- **The aperture**, how wide the hole is.
- **ISO**, how much the camera turns up what it caught.

Each one goes in steps, and every step is one **stop**: twice as much
light, or half as much. A step longer in time, 1/125 to 1/60, is one stop.
A step wider in aperture, F5.6 to F4, is one stop. A step up in ISO, 200
to 400, is one stop. That is the secret that makes it all fit together: a
stop is a stop, whichever of the three it comes from.

![Exposure as a seesaw: the light the picture needs on one side, and on the other the time, the aperture and ISO](exposure-seesaw.svg)

So the same brightness can be reached in many ways. Take one stop off the
time and put it on the aperture, and the seesaw stays level. All of these
make exactly as bright a picture:

- 1/500 at F2.8
- 1/250 at F4
- 1/125 at F5.6
- 1/60 at F8

What changes is everything else. At 1/500 a running dog is frozen, but
F2.8 leaves only the dog sharp and melts the background. At 1/60 the dog
is blurred, but F8 makes the whole garden sharp. Choosing a picture is
choosing which of these you want.

> [think] The brightness is the same. The picture is not. That is why
> photographers care about the three numbers and not only about whether
> the picture is too dark.

## When the camera says it cannot

Sometimes there is no good way. In the dark, even the widest aperture and
the longest time the camera allows are not enough, and in blinding sun
even the smallest aperture and the shortest time are too much. Then the
camera shows its numbers **blinking**, to say *"I cannot make this right"*.

![The screen in the dark: the time, 60 seconds, and the aperture, F1.8, blink](screen-too-dark.svg)

::: try
1. Set the camera to P, so that it chooses the pair of time and aperture.
2. Point it at something outdoors and read the two numbers.
3. Turn the ring that changes them together: the time gets shorter and the
   aperture wider, or the time longer and the aperture smaller, and the
   picture on the screen stays as bright.
4. Take three pictures with three different pairs, of something with a
   background and, if you can, something moving.
5. Compare the backgrounds and the moving thing.
:::

::: camera
In **P** mode the camera chooses the time and the aperture, and the
**control ring** shifts the pair: both numbers change together, the
picture stays as bright, and a small **s** appears next to the P to show
it, **Ps** (manual, p. 15). Turn the ring back until the s goes away to
let the camera choose again. This does not work with the flash on.

If the subject is too dark, the time and the aperture blink, for instance
**60" F1.8**: use the flash. If it is too bright, they blink as **2000
F8**: switch on the ND filter (manual, p. 15).
:::

## Keep trying

- Write the three numbers of five of your pictures on a piece of paper:
  time, aperture and ISO. Can you find two that let in the same light?
- Take a picture in P, then take the same in A and in S. Did the camera
  choose the same pair each time?
- Ask an adult to help you work out: if 1/125 at F5.6 is right, what time
  goes with F8? And with F2.8?
