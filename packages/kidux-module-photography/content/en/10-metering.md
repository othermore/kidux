# How the camera measures light

Before every picture the camera measures how bright the scene is, with its
**light meter**, and from that it chooses the time, the aperture or the
ISO. But here is the thing to know about every light meter ever made: **it
does not know what it is looking at.** It does not know snow from coal.
So it does the one thing it can: it makes whatever it measures come out as
a **middle grey**, not dark, not bright.

For a garden, a street, a face, that is just right: most scenes, all mixed
together, average out to about middle grey. But point the camera at snow,
and the camera makes the snow middle grey: dull, dirty-looking snow. Point
it at a black cat, and it makes the cat grey too.

![The same snow given almost two stops more light: white, as it was](snow-white.jpg)

> [point] The camera wants grey. Snow and white walls need **+1** or
> **+2**. Black things need **−1**. Now you know why the knob of chapter 5
> exists.

## Three ways of measuring

The camera can be told *where* to measure.

![The whole picture, cleverly weighed; the middle more than the edges; one small spot](metering-patterns.svg)

- **The whole picture, cleverly weighed.** The camera cuts the picture into
  pieces, measures each one and compares them with thousands of pictures
  it knows. It is right most of the time, and it is the one to leave on.
- **The middle more than the edges.** The old, simple way: everything
  counts, but the centre counts most.
- **One small spot.** The camera measures only a tiny circle in the middle
  of the screen, and nothing else. It is the way to measure one thing
  exactly: a face against a bright window, the moon in a black sky, a
  singer under a spotlight.

Here is a cat sitting in front of a bright window. Measuring the whole
picture, the camera saw so much bright window that it made the window
right and left the cat dark. Measuring a spot on the cat, the cat is right,
and the window, which does not matter, is white.

## Measuring one thing, framing another

The camera measures when you press the shutter button half-way, and keeps
that measurement for as long as you hold it there. So you can point the
middle of the screen at what matters, press half-way, and then move the
camera to frame the picture as you like, still holding, and press the rest
of the way. Some cameras also have a button that keeps the measurement
without holding.

::: try
1. Set the camera to P.
2. Fill the whole screen with a sheet of white paper and take a picture.
   Fill it with a dark jumper and take one. Are they both grey?
3. Ask a friend to stand in front of a bright window. Take a picture
   measuring the whole picture.
4. Switch to measuring one spot, put the middle of the screen on your
   friend's face, press half-way, frame, and take it.
5. Compare the faces, and look at the histogram of each.
:::

::: camera
Press **OK** for the live control and go down to the metering row (manual,
p. 35 and 42). Its five choices are: **ESP**, the whole picture cleverly
weighed, which is the one for most pictures; **centre-weighted**; **spot**;
**spot HI**, a spot that keeps a bright thing bright; and **spot SH**, a
spot that keeps a dark thing dark (manual, p. 42).

![The live control's metering row, with spot chosen: only the small circle in the middle is measured](screen-metering.svg)

The camera measures with the button half-way and keeps it while you hold
it (manual, p. 42). The **Fn1** button can be made to keep the
measurement, **AEL**, without holding the shutter button (manual, p. 65).
The camera can also measure the spot where its focus square is, instead
of the middle, once **[···]+Metering** is switched on in the menus
(manual, p. 61). For snow and white things the manual itself says to
turn the exposure compensation to + (manual, p. 81).
:::

## Keep trying

- Zoom all the way in, measure the moon with the spot and turn the +/− to
  **−2**. Then measure with the whole picture, at 0. Which moon shows its
  grey seas, and which is a white blob?
- Take a picture of a white cat or a white car with ESP, at 0 and at +1.
- In a theatre or a school play, with the stage lit and the hall dark, try
  spot on the actor's face.

::: adult
This chapter explains the light meter's fundamental assumption, that the
scene averages to middle grey, why it fails on predominantly white or
black subjects, and the three metering patterns. The snow and cat pairs are
each one photograph: the "as measured" picture is the original, or the
original made 1.7 stops darker, which is what an averaging meter does to
white snow; the other is given the light it needed.

The exposure lock, by half-press or by a button, prepares chapter 12,
working in manual, where the measurement is taken once and kept on
purpose.
:::
