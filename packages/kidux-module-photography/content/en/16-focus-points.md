# Focus: where the camera looks

The camera can make only one distance perfectly sharp at a time: the toy
at one metre, or the tree at ten, not both. Choosing that distance is
called **focusing**, and the camera does it by itself, but it does not
know what you are thinking of. It focuses on **what is under its little
square**, and nothing else.

::: compare
![Focused in front, on the toothbrushes: the shelf behind is soft](focus-front.jpg)

![Focused behind, on the shelf: now the toothbrushes are the soft ones](focus-back.jpg)

## Which square

The camera has many little squares across the picture, the **focus
targets**, and it can use them in three ways:

- **All of them.** The camera chooses, and it usually chooses the
  **nearest** thing, or the one in the middle. Fine when there is one
  thing to photograph, and it is close.
- **One.** You choose the square, and move it with the arrows to where
  the thing is. The camera looks there and nowhere else.
- **A group.** You choose a small block of squares, and the camera chooses
  inside it.

> [think] The camera does not focus on what is important. It focuses on
> what is under the square. Telling it where to look is your job.

## Focus, then frame

There is an old trick that works on every camera, even with just the
square in the middle. Put the square on the thing, press the button
**half-way**, and hold it: the camera focuses, the square turns green,
it beeps. Now, **still holding**, move the camera until the picture is
framed as you like, with the thing at the side. Then press the rest of
the way. The focus stays where you put it.

## Faces, all by themselves

Many cameras look for **faces** and focus on them without being told: a
white frame appears round each face they find. Some go further and find
the **eye** nearest the camera, which is what a portrait needs sharp.

![The camera has found a face, and the nearer eye](screen-face.svg)

::: try
1. Put two toys on a table, one near the camera and one about a metre
   behind it.
2. With **all** the targets, press half-way. Which toy did the square
   turn green on?
3. Choose **one** target, move it onto the far toy, and take the picture.
4. Choose the middle target. Put it on a toy, press half-way, hold it,
   move the camera until the toy is at the edge of the picture, and
   press the rest of the way. Is the toy sharp?
5. Tap the screen on each toy in turn, and watch where it focuses.
:::

::: camera
Press the **left** arrow, the focus target button, to see the targets:
the arrows move one target, and **INFO** chooses all of them, one, or a
group. There are 35 of them, 7 across and 5 down (manual, p. 26). Focus lock, half-way and hold, is on p. 14. The target can be
sent back to the middle with a button set up in the menus (manual, p. 59
and 65).

On the touch screen, a tap focuses where you tapped, or focuses and
takes the picture, as its icon on the screen is set (manual, p. 33). **Face priority** and **eye priority** are chosen in the live
control that OK opens (manual, p. 44). In the dark, a small light on the
front, the **AF illuminator**, lights the scene to help the camera
focus (manual, p. 59).
:::

## Keep trying

- Photograph a toy through the bars of a fence or a stair rail, with all
  the targets. What did the camera focus on? Now with one.
- Point the camera at a plain white wall, and press half-way. Does it
  manage to focus?
- Ask a friend to stand at the side of the picture, and use face
  priority. Did the white frame find them?

::: adult
This chapter teaches that autofocus focuses on the area under the active
target, not on the subject the photographer intends, and the ways of
choosing that area: all targets, a single one, a group, focus lock and
recompose, touch, and face and eye detection.

Autofocus works by finding contrast, and so it fails on plain walls, sky,
glass, bars in front of the subject, very fast movement and darkness; the
manual's own list is on p. 80. The remedy is the same in each case: focus
on something else at the same distance, hold the button half-way, and
reframe, or focus by hand (chapter 17).
:::
