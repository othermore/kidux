# A box with a hole

Every camera ever made is a dark box with a hole in one side. Yours
too, under all its buttons. To see why that is enough to make a
picture, you need one fact about light: **light travels in straight
lines**. It never bends round a corner by itself.

Imagine a room with the curtains shut and one tiny hole in them. Light
from the top of the tree outside comes through the hole and keeps going
straight, so it lands low on the wall. Light from the ground comes
through the same hole and lands high. Light from the left lands on the
right. Every point outside sends its light through the hole to one
point on the wall, and the wall shows the street: whole, in colour, and
**upside down**.

![Light from the tree crosses at the hole and draws the tree upside down on the wall](camera-obscura.svg)

> [point] Upside down is not a mistake. It is the proof that light goes
> straight. Turn the drawing round in your head: the top of the tree can
> only reach the bottom of the wall.

People noticed this long before anyone could keep the picture. A
Chinese thinker called Mozi wrote it down about two thousand four
hundred years ago. Fourteen hundred years later, Ibn al-Haytham in Cairo used
a dark room to study light. Painters used such rooms to trace what they
saw. The Latin name stuck: **camera obscura**, "dark room". That is
where the word *camera* comes from. Your camera is a very small dark
room.

![The first printed picture of a camera obscura: an eclipse of the sun watched on the wall of a dark room, 1545](frisius-1545.jpg)

::: try
1. Choose a room with one window and a view: a street, a garden, trees.
   Do this on a bright day.
2. Cover the window completely with cardboard or a thick blanket. Tape
   the edges. It has to be really dark.
3. Make one hole in the cardboard, in the middle, about as big as a
   pencil.
4. Hold a white sheet or a big piece of paper about a metre from the
   hole, facing it.
5. Wait. Your eyes need five or ten minutes to get used to the dark.
   Then look at the sheet.

You will see the view outside, faint, in its colours, upside down. Move
the sheet nearer the hole and further away. Make the hole bigger with a
finger and watch what happens to the picture.
:::

What did you see when the hole got bigger? The picture got brighter,
and blurrier. A small hole gives a sharp picture with very little
light. A big hole gives lots of light and a fuzzy picture. Keep that in
your pocket: the next chapter is about how a piece of glass gets you
both at once.

::: camera
Take the cap off the lens and look at the front of your camera. The
glass is the hole, made better. Behind it, inside the body, is a small
dark room with a wall at the back that remembers light: the **sensor**.
On the XZ-2 it is about the size of your little fingernail, and it is
made of twelve million tiny squares that each count how much light
landed on them (manual, p. 92). When you press the button, the picture
on that wall is kept, the right way up, because the camera turns it
over for you.
:::

> [think] If a hole can make a picture, why does a camera need all the
> rest? Hold that question. Each chapter answers one piece of it.

## Keep trying

- Instead of a room, use a shoebox: a pinhole in one end, and tracing
  paper over the other end, for its far wall. The picture shows through
  the paper, upside down, as the drawing shows; a blanket over your head
  and the box keeps the rest of the light out. Point the pinhole at the
  window, or at a lamp.
- Stand under a leafy tree on a sunny day and look at the ground. The
  spots of light between the shadows are round. Every tiny gap between
  the leaves is a hole, and each spot is a picture of the sun.
- Hold your camera upside down and take a photograph. Is the photograph
  upside down?

::: adult
This chapter teaches one idea: light travels in straight lines, so a
hole in a dark box projects an inverted image. Everything later in the
course rests on it. The darkened room is the experiment worth the
trouble; a child who has seen their own street upside down on a bedsheet
understands cameras differently from one who has read about it. It
needs a very dark room (tape the edges of the cardboard), a bright day,
and patience while the eyes adapt. The image is dim because the hole is
small; that trade between brightness and sharpness is exactly what
chapter 2 (the lens) and chapter 7 (the aperture) are about.

The course names the Olympus XZ-2 in boxes like the one above, because
it is the camera it was written with. Any camera with a mode dial and a
way to change the shutter time and the aperture will do: chapter 3 says
how to find the same controls on another one.

The picture of the room is made by a computer; the shoebox is a real
photograph.
:::
