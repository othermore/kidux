# Light that moves: long exposures

Leave the shutter open for whole seconds, with the camera perfectly still,
and strange things happen. Everything that stays still comes out sharp.
Everything that moves smears into lines and clouds, or vanishes. And every
moving light **draws its path**.

![A street at night, the shutter open for seconds: the cars are gone, and their lights have drawn red and white ribbons](car-trails.jpg)

## The first photograph of a person

The first cameras needed minutes for a picture. In 1838, Louis Daguerre
pointed his at a busy boulevard in Paris, full of carriages and people
walking. In his picture, the boulevard is empty. Everything that moved
during those minutes left no mark at all. Everything but one man: he was
standing still to have his shoes shined, and so he is there, at the bottom
left, the first person ever photographed.

![Louis Daguerre's Boulevard du Temple, Paris, 1838: an empty street, and at the bottom left a man standing still to have his shoes shined](daguerre-boulevard.jpg)

## What a long time does

- **Car lights** become ribbons: white ones coming towards you, red ones
  going away.
- **Water** turns to silk or mist.
- **The stars** draw circles round the one star that does not move, the
  Pole Star, because the Earth is turning under them.
- **People walking** become ghosts, or disappear.

> [oops] The camera must not move by a hair. Put it on a tripod, a wall or
> a bean bag, and use the self-timer, so that pressing the button does not
> shake it.

::: try
1. Go out with an adult at dusk, when the street lamps are on.
2. Put the camera on a wall, a car roof or a small tripod, pointing at a
   road.
3. Set a time of **4 seconds**, then **8**, with the self-timer.
4. Take a picture each time a car passes.
5. Then point the camera at a fountain or a stream, and take it at
   **1 second**.
:::

::: camera
In **S** the time goes into whole seconds, 1" and 2" and longer (manual,
p. 17); in **M** it goes to 60 seconds, and further, to **BULB**, where
the shutter stays open for as long as you hold the button down, up to 16
minutes (manual, p. 18). The **Bulb Timer** in the menus
sets that limit (manual, p. 61). A remote cable, bought separately, lets
you hold it without touching the camera (manual, p. 18).

On a tripod, switch the **image stabilizer** off (manual, p. 36). After a
long exposure the camera takes about as long again to clean the picture's
noise, and shows that it is busy: wait for it (manual, p. 18 and 61). In
daylight, the **ND filter** takes away three stops of light, so that
water can turn to silk even in the sun (manual, p. 65 and 92). For
fireworks, SCN has a scene of its own (manual, p. 20).
:::

## Keep trying

- On a clear night far from town lights, ask an adult to help you point
  the camera at the sky for a few minutes in BULB.
- Take a picture of a fairground roundabout at night at 2 seconds.
- Ask a friend to stand very still in the middle of a busy place while you
  take 4 seconds. Who is in the picture?

::: adult
This chapter is about long exposures: static subjects sharp, moving ones
blurred or gone, moving lights drawn as trails. Daguerre's *Boulevard du
Temple* (1838) is public domain; the man having his shoes shined, at the
lower left, is the earliest known photograph of a person.

Long exposures need an adult outdoors at dusk, a stable support and a
self-timer. Star trails need a dark place, a clear sky and patience; the
battery runs down faster in the cold.
:::
