# Hold it still, press gently

Most blurred photographs are not blurred because something moved in front
of the camera. They are blurred because the **camera** moved, just a
little, while the picture was being taken. It is called **camera shake**.

While the shutter is open, every point of the world is drawing itself on
the sensor. If the camera moves, each point draws a little line instead of
a dot, and the whole picture smears. Look at the lights in this street at
night: each one has drawn the wobble of the hand that held the camera.

![A street at night, taken with a shaking camera: every light has drawn the hand's wobble](shaken-lights.jpg)

In daylight the wobble is smaller, but it is still there, and it makes a
toy look soft and fuzzy, as if seen through a dirty window.

![A toy snow machine, photographed with the camera moving](shaken-toy.jpg)

## How to hold a camera

- **Both hands.** The right hand holds the grip, with the finger on the
  shutter button; the left hand holds the camera from underneath or by
  its side.
- **Elbows in.** Tuck your elbows against your body, as if you were
  holding a book against your chest. Your body becomes a stand.
- **Feet apart**, one a little in front of the other, so that you do not
  sway.
- **Fingers away from the front**: not over the lens, and not over the
  flash.

![Holding a camera with both hands and the elbows tucked in, level and upright](holding.svg)

## Press gently, in two steps

The shutter button has two steps. Press it **half-way** and hold it: the
camera focuses and works out the light, a little square turns green on
the screen where it focused, and it beeps. Then press it **the rest of the
way**, smoothly, without jabbing, and the picture is taken.

![The screen with the button pressed half-way: the green square on the toy, and the time and aperture the camera chose](screen-half-press.svg)

> [point] Breathe out, then press. People shake least just after they
> breathe out. Marksmen and photographers both know it.

When there is very little light, the shutter has to stay open longer, and
even the best hands shake. Then let something else hold the camera: put
it on a table, a wall, a pile of books, and take the picture with the
**self-timer**, so that your finger is not touching it at all when the
shutter opens.

::: try
1. Find a lamp in a room that is not very bright.
2. Take five pictures of it holding the camera any old way, pressing the
   button fast.
3. Take five more holding it the right way: both hands, elbows in, the
   button half-way first, breathe out, the rest of the way gently.
4. Take one more with the camera on a table and the self-timer.
5. Look at all eleven on the screen, zoomed in. Which are sharp?
:::

::: camera
The XZ-2 beeps and shows the green square when it has focused with the
button half-way (manual, p. 9 and 14). Hold it as the manual shows, with
your fingers off the flash and the microphone (manual, p. 9).

It also has an **image stabilizer**, which moves the sensor against your
hand's wobble: leave it on, at IS1, when you hold the camera, and switch
it off when the camera is on a table or a tripod (manual, p. 36). It
helps, but it cannot stop a big shake.

The self-timer is under the **down** arrow: press it, then choose the
2-second timer for the table, or the 12-second one to get into the
picture yourself (manual, p. 27). To see whether a picture is sharp,
press playback and turn the zoom lever towards **T** to zoom into it, up
to 14 times (manual, p. 11). With **INFO** you can also show a level on
the screen, which tells you when the camera is straight, once an adult has
switched it on in the menus (manual, p. 60 and 69).
:::

## Keep trying

- Take a picture of a straight line, the edge of a table or the horizon,
  and look at it: is it straight in the picture? Straight lines that lean
  look like a mistake.
- Hold the camera upright, for a tall thing, with the same elbows in.
- Try leaning against a wall or a tree while you take a picture. Does it
  help?

::: adult
This chapter is about camera shake and how to avoid it: a stable grip,
elbows against the body, a smooth two-step press of the shutter button,
and support plus the self-timer when the light is low. It is the habit
that improves a beginner's pictures most. The two pictures at the top are
deliberately shaken; the comparison the child makes in *Try it* teaches
more than any picture.

A child's hands are small for many cameras; let them hold it with the
strap round the wrist. The beep's volume can be changed in the menus
(manual, p. 60).
:::
