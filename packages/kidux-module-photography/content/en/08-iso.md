# ISO: how hard the camera listens

When there is plenty of light, the sensor's millions of little squares
fill up nicely and the picture is clean. When there is very little, each
square catches only a few drops of light. The camera can still make a
bright picture: it **turns up** what each square caught, the way you turn
up the volume of a quiet radio. How much it turns it up is called **ISO**.

![The sensor's little squares. Left, plenty of light: all full. Middle, little light: a few drops each. Right, those few drops turned up: the squares look full, but unevenly](sensor-pixels.svg)

- **ISO 100** or **200**: no turning up. The cleanest picture there is,
  for daylight.
- **ISO 800**, **1600**: turned up a lot. Fine for a room with lamps.
- **ISO 6400**, **12800**: turned up as far as it goes. The camera can
  see in the dark, but the picture is full of grain.

That grain is called **noise**, and it is just like the hiss of a radio
turned up too far: tiny specks of the wrong brightness and the wrong
colour, everywhere, worst in the dark parts. Here are the same clay
figures, photographed at ISO 200 and at ISO 6400, both shown as large as
the camera made them.

::: compare
![ISO 200: smooth clay](iso-200.jpg)

![ISO 6400: the same clay, full of coloured grain](iso-6400.jpg)
:::

Every step from ISO 100 to 200 to 400 to 800 is one stop more: twice as
bright, from the same light. So ISO is the third way, after the time and
the aperture, of making a picture brighter, and it is the one with the
worst side effect. The rule is simple: **use the lowest ISO the light lets
you**, and turn it up only when the time would be too long to hold the
camera, or to freeze what moves.

> [point] When you are not sure, let the camera choose: ISO **AUTO** turns
> it up only as much as it needs.

::: try
1. Find a dim room, with one lamp on.
2. Put a toy on a table, and the camera on the table too.
3. Set ISO to **100** and take the picture with the self-timer. Look at
   the time the camera chose: it is long.
4. Set ISO to **6400** and take the picture holding the camera. Look at
   the time: it is short.
5. Zoom right into both pictures on the screen, into a dark corner.
   Which has the grain?
:::

::: camera
Press **OK** to open the live control, the row of settings down the right
of the screen; go to ISO with the up and down arrows, and choose with left
and right: **AUTO**, or a number from 100 to 12800 (manual, p. 35 and 44).
When ISO is AUTO, the XZ-2 goes no higher than 1600 by itself, unless an
adult changes that limit in the menus (manual, p. 61 and 90).

The camera also tries to clean up the grain of high ISOs, which it calls
the **noise filter** (manual, p. 61). It helps, but cleaning grain away
also wipes away a little detail.
:::

## Keep trying

- Take the same picture outdoors on a grey day at ISO 100 and at ISO
  3200, and zoom into a shadow. The grain is there outdoors too: the
  camera let in less light to make up for turning it up. ISO is never
  free.
- Find the darkest place you can take a picture of without a flash. Which
  ISO did you need?
- At night, look out of a window at the street and take it at ISO 6400.
  Where is the noise worst?

::: adult
This chapter introduces ISO as amplification of the sensor's signal, its
cost in noise, and its place as the third exposure control. The two
pictures of the clay figures are real photographs from the Natural Image
Noise Dataset, taken at ISO 200 and ISO 6400 and shown at full size, so
the noise is the camera's own.

A child should leave ISO on AUTO for most pictures and set it by hand only
in manual mode (chapter 12), where it must not change by itself.
:::
