# The shutter: how long the door is open

Behind the lens there is a little door, the **shutter**, closed all the
time except for the moment when the picture is taken. How long it stays
open is called the **shutter speed**, and it is a time, measured in
seconds and bits of seconds.

- **1/1000** of a second: imagine one second cut into a thousand slices,
  and take one. Hardly anything can move in that time, so whatever moves
  comes out **frozen**: a drop of water hanging in the air, a ball
  stopped above the ground.
- **1/60**: an ordinary indoor picture. A walking person is sharp, a
  running dog may be a little blurred.
- **1/8** or **1/4**: moving things become smears. Water turns to silk,
  and a hand waving becomes a cloud.
- **1 second**, **2 seconds** and more: only still things stay sharp, and
  the camera must not move at all.

On the screen the camera writes only the bottom of the fraction: **125**
means 1/125 of a second, and **1000** means 1/1000. Whole seconds have a
little mark after them: **2"** is two seconds.

> [point] A short time freezes, a long time blurs. A blur is not always a
> mistake: it is how a still picture shows that something moved.

## The horse with its feet in the air

For thousands of years, people argued about whether a galloping horse
ever has all four feet off the ground at once. It moves too fast for an
eye to see. In 1878 a photographer called Eadweard Muybridge set up a row
of cameras along a racetrack, each with a very fast shutter, and a thread
for the horse to break as it passed each one. The pictures settled it: in
one of them, the horse is flying.

![Eadweard Muybridge's horse, 1878: twelve pictures, each a fraction of a second after the one before; in some of them the horse has all four feet in the air](muybridge-horse.jpg)

::: try
1. Turn on a tap so that the water runs fast.
2. Take a picture at **1/1000**, with the camera held well.
3. Take one at **1/8**, with the camera on the side of the sink.
4. Ask a friend to wave a hand slowly, and take it at **1/500** and at
   **1/15**.
5. Look out of a window at the street and take the cars at **1/4**, the
   camera resting on the window sill.
:::

::: camera
Set the mode dial to **S**, shutter priority: you choose the time, and
the camera chooses the rest (manual, p. 17). The **control ring**, round
the lens, changes the time: slower one way, 2", 1", 15, faster the other,
60, 100, 400, 1000. The sub dial still changes the exposure compensation.

If the time you choose lets in too little light or too much, the camera
cannot make the picture right, and the aperture's number at the bottom of
the screen **blinks** (manual, p. 17). The XZ-2's times go from 1/2000 of
a second to 60 seconds (manual, p. 18).
:::

## Keep trying

- Throw a ball up in the air and take it at 1/1000. Then at 1/30.
- Take a picture of a spinning top or a bicycle's wheel at different
  times. When does it stop looking as if it moves?
- Follow a moving bicycle with the camera, turning with it, at 1/30: the
  bicycle can come out sharp and the background blurred. This is called
  **panning**.

::: adult
This chapter introduces the shutter speed as a fraction of a second, and
its two effects: freezing or blurring movement. Children who have not yet
met fractions understand "a second cut into a thousand pieces" well. At
slow speeds the camera itself must be still (chapter 4).

Muybridge's sequence (1878) is in the public domain; his experiment with
twelve cameras is a good story for a child who likes horses or science.
:::
