# Why a lens

In the dark room of chapter 1 you found a problem. A small hole gives a
sharp picture, but a dim one. A big hole gives a bright picture, but a
blurred one. Why blurred?

Think of one bright dot outside, a lamp. Through a tiny hole, its light
arrives on the wall as a tiny dot. Through a big hole, its light spreads
as it comes through and arrives as a big round patch. Every point of the
world becomes a patch, the patches overlap, and the whole picture is a
blur.

A **lens** fixes that. A lens is a piece of glass that is thicker in the
middle than at the edges, and glass shaped like that **bends light**. The
light from the lamp comes through all of the big hole, spread out, and
the lens bends it back together, so that it meets again at one single
point. Now the hole can be big, for lots of light, and the picture is
still sharp.

![Above, a big hole: the lamp's light lands as a patch. Below, a lens in the hole: it lands as a point](lens-focus.svg)

> [point] The place where the light meets again is the place where the
> picture is sharp. Nearer than that, or further, and the dot is a patch
> again. Finding that place is called **focusing**.

There is a catch. The light from a far tree and the light from your hand
meet at different distances behind the lens. A lens can make only one
distance sharp at a time. That is why a camera has to focus, and why it
makes a small noise before every picture: it is moving its glass until the
thing you are pointing at is sharp.

Your eyes do the same, all day, without asking you. Each eye has a lens
inside it, and a muscle that squeezes it a little for near things and lets
it go for far things. Look at your finger, then at the window, then at your
finger again: feel the little change? That is your eye focusing.

::: try
1. Find a magnifying glass, a sheet of white paper, and a room with a
   bright window. Switch off the lights in the room.
2. Stand with your back to the side wall, the window on one side of you,
   the paper on the other. Hold the paper so that it faces the window.
3. Hold the magnifying glass between the window and the paper, about a
   hand and a half from the paper.
4. Move the glass slowly nearer to the paper and further away until the
   window appears on the paper, small and sharp.
:::

![A magnifying glass, on the right, draws the bright window on the paper, small and upside down: its bright panes are at the bottom](magnifier-window.jpg)

The window is upside down again, just like in the dark room. A lens is
still a hole, only a better one. Now measure how far the glass is from the
paper when the window is sharp. Then point the glass at a lamp on the other
side of the room, much nearer, and find its sharp place: it is further from
the glass. A near thing comes to a point further back. That is exactly what
the camera's lens moves for.

> [oops] Never point a magnifying glass at the sun, and never look at the
> sun through one. It gathers so much light into its point that it burns.

::: camera
The lens is the glass tube at the front of the camera. Inside it there is
not one glass but several, working together. The zoom lever, round the
shutter button on top, makes the lens longer and shorter: turn it towards
**T** and far things come nearer, towards **W** and more fits in (manual,
p. 10). Chapter 20 is about what the zoom does to a picture.

Keep the lens cap on when you are not taking pictures, and tie it to the
camera with its little strap so that it is never lost (manual, p. 4). A
fingerprint on the glass makes every picture foggy. Blow dust off first,
then wipe the glass very gently with a soft lens cloth (manual, p. 82).
:::

## Keep trying

- Use the magnifying glass on a sunny day indoors: point it at the window
  and put the paper on the far wall. How big is the sharp window now?
- Look through the magnifying glass at a far tree, with your arm
  stretched out. The tree is upside down there too. Bring the glass slowly
  closer to your eye: when does it turn the right way up?
- Fill a clear glass with water and look at a pencil behind it. Water
  shaped like that is a lens too.

::: adult
This chapter explains why cameras have lenses: a converging lens brings
the light from one point of the scene back to one point, so a large
opening can be used without blur, but it does so at one distance only,
hence focusing. The magnifying-glass experiment needs a dim room and a
bright window; the image is faint, and a white card held steady helps.
The warning about the sun matters: a magnifier focused on the sun can
burn skin, paper and eyes.

The idea that "near things focus further back" prepares chapter 16 and
17 (focusing) and chapter 18 (depth of field), where the child sees that
only one distance is ever perfectly sharp.
:::
